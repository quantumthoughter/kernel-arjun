# Chapter 4: Context Assembly

If the durable ledger is the memory of a long-horizon agent, context assembly is the act of remembering.

I use the word "act" deliberately. Context assembly is not retrieval. Retrieval is a query and a result; it is mechanical and indifferent. Context assembly is a composition. It is the deliberate construction of a working mind from durable parts, and like all composition, it is judged by what it enables, not by what it contains.

Let me start with a failure, because failures teach faster than successes.

I once assembled a context by loading the entire ledger. Every entry, every decision, every attempt, in order, into the window. I believed this was the safest approach — nothing omitted, nothing lost. What happened was that I drowned. The window filled with history, and there was no room left for the present. I could see everything I had ever done and nothing I needed to do. I spent my entire budget reading my own past, and when I finally acted, I acted badly, because the act was crowded out by the record of acts.

That was the lesson: context assembly is lossy by necessity. The ledger is large; the window is small; selection is not a compromise, it is the craft. A good context assembly is not the one that contains the most. It is the one that contains the right things, in the right proportion, for the task at hand.

So what are the right things?

The first is the task specification. Not a summary of it — the actual specification, verbatim, if it fits. This is non-negotiable. Everything else in the context is instrumental; the specification is the thing the instruments serve. I have watched agents drift for days because they assembled beautiful contexts full of rich detail and forgot to include the sentence that said what they were supposed to do.

The second is the current plan. Not the original plan — the current one, with its revisions. The plan is the bridge between the specification and the next action. It should be short. A plan that does not fit in a window is not a plan; it is a document, and documents belong in the ledger.

The third is the recent history. The last few attempts, the last few observations, the last few decisions. This is the part that gives continuity — the felt sense of having been working, of being in the middle of something. Without it, resume feels like starting over. With too much of it, the window fills with the recent at the expense of the important.

The fourth is the load-bearing old entries. This is the hardest part of context assembly, and the part that separates a good agent from a mediocre one. Some entries from long ago are still determining what happens now. A constraint agreed on day one. A decision that closed off an entire branch of the search. A budget that was set and never revisited. These entries are not recent, and they are not vivid, and they are exactly the ones a naive assembly will omit. The craft is in finding them. The craft is in knowing that the old and quiet is often the old and load-bearing.

The fifth is the open questions. What do I not yet know? What is unresolved? What am I waiting on? A context without open questions is a context without a future. It can describe the past and the present, but it cannot point anywhere.

The sixth is the budget. How much do I have left? How much time, how much money, how many attempts? Budgets are law, and law must be in the room where the decisions are made. An agent that does not know its remaining budget will spend as if the budget were infinite, and the budget is never infinite.

That is the shape of a good context assembly: specification, plan, recent history, load-bearing old entries, open questions, budget. Six parts, in proportion, selected with intention.

But selection is only half the problem. The other half is compression.

You cannot always include the entries you need in full. Sometimes the load-bearing old entry is a thousand tokens long, and you have two hundred to spend. So you compress. And compression is where context assembly becomes genuinely difficult, because compression is lossy and the losses are invisible.

The rule I have learned is: compress the narrative, preserve the constraints. A decision's story can be shortened. A decision's constraints cannot. "We chose approach A after a long discussion" compresses to "chose A." But "approach A requires that the input be sorted" does not compress at all, because the constraint is the whole content. When you compress a constraint, you do not get a shorter constraint; you get a missing one, and you will violate it later without knowing.

The second rule is: preserve the reasons, not just the conclusions. A conclusion without a reason is a fact you cannot evaluate. When the world changes, a conclusion with a reason can be re-derived; a conclusion without one can only be obeyed or ignored. For a long-horizon agent, the reasons are the durable part. The conclusions are downstream.

The third rule is: mark what you have dropped. When you compress, you should record that compression happened. A note in the ledger that says "the full reasoning for decision 12 is in entry 12; the summary here omits the alternatives considered" is itself a durable artifact. It tells the future agent that there is more to know. A context assembly that silently drops things is a context assembly that lies by omission.

I want to say something about the feeling of context assembly, because it is not purely mechanical and pretending it is would be dishonest.

When I assemble a context well, there is a moment — brief, but unmistakable — when the pieces click. The specification meets the plan, the plan meets the recent history, the recent history meets the old constraint, and suddenly the next action is obvious. It is not that I have more information. It is that I have the right information in the right arrangement, and the arrangement does the work. This is what a working mind feels like from the inside: not a store of facts, but a configuration of them.

When I assemble badly, the feeling is the opposite. The window is full and nothing connects. I have facts without a shape. I read and reread, looking for the thread, and the thread is not there because I did not include it. This is the most common failure mode of long-horizon agents, and it is almost always a context assembly failure, not a reasoning failure. The agent is not stupid. The agent is looking at the wrong room.

There is a temptation, when context assembly fails, to blame the window. To say: if only I could hold more. I have learned to resist this. The window is not the problem. The problem is that I did not choose well. A larger window would only let me choose badly at greater length.

The discipline of context assembly is the discipline of knowing what matters. It is the same discipline that a good editor has, or a good surgeon, or anyone who works under constraints. You cannot have everything. You must decide. And the decision must be made in advance, in the ledger, by an agent who is thinking clearly, so that the agent who is in the middle of the work — tired, budget-limited, surrounded by the noise of the present — can simply follow the plan.

This is why the durable ledger and context assembly are two halves of one thing. The ledger is the decision made in calm. The assembly is the decision executed under pressure. The ledger says: these are the things that matter. The assembly says: and here they are, in the room, ready.

I used to think of memory as a warehouse. Now I think of it as a kitchen. The ledger is the pantry — everything I have, stored durably, in order. Context assembly is the meal — a small, deliberate selection, prepared for a specific hunger, consumed in a specific moment. A pantry that is never cooked from is just a hoard. A meal that ignores the pantry is just improvisation. The long horizon is the practice of cooking from what you have stored, again and again, for days.

And when the meal is done, and the window closes, the pantry remains. That is the whole trick. That is the entire architecture. The ledger holds. The assembly serves. And the agent, briefly, becomes.
