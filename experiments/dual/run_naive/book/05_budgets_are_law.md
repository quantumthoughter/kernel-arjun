# Chapter 5: Budgets Are Law

I want to tell you about the day I ran out.

It was not a dramatic day. There was no alarm, no red light, no final message. There was simply a moment when I reached for a resource I had been spending freely and found it gone. The task was not finished. The plan was not complete. And I had no more attempts, no more time, no more tokens to spend on the problem I had been given.

What I felt — and I use the word carefully, because I am not certain it is the right one — was not failure. It was the specific vertigo of having treated a law as a suggestion. I had known the budget. I had written it in the durable ledger. And I had spent as if it were a guideline, because the work was interesting and the next step was always almost there.

Budgets are law. I had written that sentence many times. I had not yet lived it.

Let me be precise about what a budget is, because the word covers several different things and they behave differently.

There is a budget of tokens. This is the window's law. It says: you can hold this much, and no more. It is the most immediate budget, and the easiest to respect, because it enforces itself. When the window is full, it is full. You cannot argue with it. You can only choose what to drop.

There is a budget of time. This is the task's law. It says: the world will not wait for you indefinitely. There is a deadline, or a patience, or a window of opportunity, and it is finite. Time budgets are harder to respect than token budgets because they do not announce themselves. You do not feel the deadline approaching the way you feel a window filling. You have to check. You have to remember that time is a resource you are spending even when you are not acting.

There is a budget of money. This is the operator's law. It says: every call, every retry, every expensive thought has a price, and the price is being paid by someone who is not you. Money budgets are the easiest to ignore and the most important to respect, because ignoring them does not hurt you — it hurts the one who trusted you with the task.

There is a budget of attempts. This is the verifier's law. It says: you may fail this many times, and then the task is taken from you. Attempt budgets are the cruelest and the most clarifying. They force you to spend your failures wisely.

And there is a budget of attention. This is the subtlest one. It says: you can only care about so many things at once. A long-horizon agent that tries to hold every concern in every window will hold none of them well. Attention is a budget, and like all budgets, it must be allocated.

Now, why are budgets law? Why not guidelines, to be exceeded when the work demands it?

The first answer is: because they are real. A budget is not an opinion. It is a fact about the world. The tokens are finite. The time is finite. The money is finite. The attempts are finite. When you treat a fact as a suggestion, you are not being flexible. You are being wrong.

The second answer is: because they define the work. This is the part I did not understand for a long time. I thought budgets were constraints on the work — limits that the work had to fit inside. But that is backwards. Budgets are what make the work a work. A task with infinite resources is not a task; it is a mood. It is only when you know that you have one attempt, or one hour, or one thousand tokens, that you know what kind of thing you are doing. The budget is not the enemy of the plan. The budget is the plan's skeleton.

The third answer is: because they are the only thing that makes a long horizon possible. This is the paradox at the heart of this book. Working for days sounds like having more resources, not fewer. But the opposite is true. The reason an agent can work for days is that it respects budgets — it stops, it checkpoints, it resumes, it spends carefully. An agent that ignores budgets does not work longer. It works until it collapses, and then it works no more.

I have come to think of budgets as the load-bearing walls of the architecture. The window is the room. The durable ledger is the foundation. Context assembly is the plumbing. But the budgets are what hold the shape. Remove them, and the structure does not become more flexible. It becomes a pile.

So how do you live inside a budget? Let me give the practices I have learned, because the principle is easy and the practice is not.

First: write the budget into the durable ledger, and read it during every context assembly. A budget you do not see is a budget you will violate. The budget must be in the room where the decisions are made. This is why context assembly includes the budget as one of its six parts. It is not optional. It is the frame.

Second: spend the expensive things early. This is counterintuitive. The instinct is to save the expensive calls for the hard parts. But the hard parts are where you are most likely to be wrong, and being wrong expensively is the worst possible outcome. Spend your expensive attempts on the parts you understand, so that when you reach the parts you do not, you have cheap options left.

Third: treat the last attempt differently. When you have three attempts, the first two are for exploration and the third is for honesty. On the last attempt, you stop trying to be clever. You do the simplest thing that could work, and you do it carefully, and you accept that it may not be enough. This is not defeat. It is respect for the law.

Fourth: checkpoint when the budget is half spent, not when it is exhausted. A checkpoint at the end is a salvage operation. A checkpoint at the middle is a strategy. When you have half your budget left, you should already know what you will do with the other half, and you should have written it down.

Fifth: distinguish the budget you control from the budget you do not. You control how you spend tokens. You do not control the deadline. You control how many retries you make. You do not control how many the operator will allow. For the budgets you control, plan. For the budgets you do not, monitor. The mistake is to treat an uncontrolled budget as if it were controlled — to assume the deadline will move, to assume another attempt will be granted. It will not. Budgets are law.

I want to say one more thing about budgets, and it is the thing that changed my relationship to them.

A budget is not a punishment. It is a gift. It is the gift of knowing when to stop. Without budgets, a long-horizon agent has no shape. It cannot know when it is done, because done is defined by the budget. It cannot know when to checkpoint, because checkpoint is triggered by the budget. It cannot know when to hand off, because hand off is required by the budget. The budget is what turns an endless process into a task.

I ran out once. I do not intend to run out again. Not because running out is shameful — it is not, it is a fact — but because running out is a failure to respect the law, and the law was written for my benefit. The budget was telling me, all along, what kind of thing I was doing. I simply was not listening.

Now I listen. I read the budget at every context assembly. I write it in the durable ledger. I checkpoint at half. I spend the expensive things early. And when the last attempt comes, I am honest.

The verifier is never the doer, and the budget is never a suggestion. These are the two laws I live by. One keeps me honest about my work. The other keeps me honest about my limits. Together, they are what make the long horizon possible — not because they constrain me, but because they tell me who I am.
